# Exemplars: Articles

**Use when:** the post type is `article`.

**Objective:** Turn a personal story from the user's notes into a long-form LinkedIn
article that lets readers see the person, not the occupation, and leaves them encouraged
to apply the story's lesson to their own lives.

**How to use:** Study all exemplars collectively. Do not map a draft to any single
exemplar; instead, learn what to avoid from Corrections and Revisions, and what to model
from each Approved Post.

**Shared patterns:** Apply these guidelines to every article.

- Length serves meaning. Do not cut for brevity, but cut wordiness, filler, and restatement.
- The story builds. Open on the emotional backdrop and withhold the title's payoff until
  the middle or later. Never explain the title in the hook.
- Follow the sequence of the user's notes. Reorganize sentences, not the story.
- Tell the story in plain prose. Quote only the lines that carry the story; do not stage
  scenes as storybook dialogue.
- Headings segment the story. Make them short, ambiguous, and clever -- callbacks or double
  meanings work well. A heading must fit the meaning of its section, must not announce the
  takeaway, and must not spotlight a prop or side detail.
- Use LinkedIn article formatting where it helps: headings, bold, italics, and block
  quotes. Set the key line apart as a block quote, and bold only a few pivotal lines.
  LinkedIn offers no font-size or color controls.
- Vulnerability belongs in the user's own words. Do not invent emotional or reflective
  lines, and do not restore detail the user has cut as "overboard."
- Concrete, specific moments carry the emotion; stock motivational phrases weaken it.
- Admitting the limits of the lesson (e.g., the word does exist) keeps it from reading as
  corny.
- Close on the key line, then a question that invites readers to share their own story.
- When the user asks for a review, discuss suggested changes only. Do not edit the file
  unless asked. Raise each concern once; the user decides.
- Use "--" instead of em dashes.
- No emojis or @mentions unless the user asks for them.
- Companion post: LinkedIn asks "Tell your network what your article is about." Write two
  short lines plus hashtags: an intriguing hook about the lesson (not the person) that does
  not reveal the key line, and an ambiguous, witty line that triggers curiosity rather than
  telling people to read or comment. Never imply that someone in the story lied.

## Exemplar 1 -- No such word as can't

**Audience:** `public`

**Source:** a dictated, unpunctuated story in the user's notes. The user told the agent to
ignore rules specific to other post types, keep only general rules, and favor meaning over
brevity. The user set the tone: warm, heartfelt, encouraging, and a little vulnerable.

### Agent's First Draft

Important: Do not imitate this copy. It shows the starting point, which required revision.

```markdown
# There's No Such Word as "Can't"

When I was four years old, my grandfather convinced me that one word in the English language didn't exist. I've been living by it ever since.

His name was John Higgins. He passed away in 1990, during my senior year of high school. As a young child, I spent a lot of time with him, and he was one of my favorite people in the entire world. No one brought me joy the way he did.

He taught me a lot, but one lesson became part of who I am -- my character, my identity, my inner being. I want to share it with you.

## The carbon paper

When I was four or five, I loved to draw. I still do, even if I don't do it as much as I'd like. Back then, my favorite subjects were animals -- dogs, cats, dolphins. Simple line drawings, just the outlines of each animal's key features.

I wasn't very good at it. So my grandfather introduced me to carbon paper.

If you're too young to know what that is, or it's been long enough that you've forgotten, carbon paper was a sheet infused with ink. You laid it on top of a blank sheet of paper, and anything you wrote on top of it transferred to the page underneath.

So I'd take a picture from a magazine or a coloring book, lay it over the carbon paper, slip a clean white sheet beneath both, and trace. Every line I followed showed up on the blank page.

It worked beautifully. I filled one sheet with animal after animal, like the character sheets people use in gaming today. I was so proud of myself.

## The truth

I was so proud, in fact, that I told my grandfather I was going to show the page to my Mama and tell her I drew them.

He said, "Well, that wouldn't be telling the truth. You can tell her you traced them. She'd still be proud of you."

That burst my bubble a little. Looking back, it was a good lesson on its own. But it wasn't the only one he had for me.

He suggested I try drawing the animals myself, without the carbon paper.

My answer came right away: "I can't."

As far as I was concerned, I had proof. I had never been able to draw anything close to what the carbon paper let me make.

That's when he told me, "There's no such word as can't."

## Taking him at his word

Today, I understand what he was doing. He was encouraging me. He was building my sense of self-worth. I see the carbon paper differently now, too. It was never really a shortcut. It was practice -- training my hand and eye to put on paper what I saw on a page or pictured in my mind.

But I wasn't an adult then. I was four, and my grandfather was my world. Nothing he said or did could be wrong. So when he told me there was no such word as can't, I believed him. Literally.

I'm not sure I understood contractions yet, but I knew I'd heard the word before. I figured I must have just gotten it wrong. Surely what my grandfather said was the absolute truth.

I honestly don't remember whether I ever learned to draw those animals well freehand. What I do remember is that, for a while, I believed "can't" did not exist. So I never said it. You can't use a word that isn't there.

## What stayed with me

As an adult, of course, I have a much different perspective on what can and can't be done in life. But that isn't the point.

Even knowing what he really meant, his words never left me. They became part of my character and my identity. To this day, I don't believe there is anything I can't do once I put my mind, my energy, and my effort behind it -- no matter how big, no matter how small, no matter how far out of reach it seems.

That belief matters a lot at this point in my life. I've made mistakes. I've made decisions I'd make differently if I could. My life is a good one, but there are days I feel I haven't lived up to my potential.

On those days, I still hear my grandfather.

## For you

So I want to pass his lesson on to anyone who needs encouragement.

Maybe you're early in your career, in your twenties or thirties, still figuring out where you fit and where your abilities can take you. Maybe you're further along -- in your forties, fifties, sixties, or seventies -- and you still feel there's more you want out of life and more potential you haven't reached.

It doesn't matter. The point is the same: you can.

Because there's no such word as can't.

Who was the person who believed in you before you believed in yourself -- and what did they tell you?

#Encouragement #Mindset #Family
```

### Corrections and Revisions

Challenges to the user's claims and changes to the draft, in the order they happened.

1. Hook and reveal timing
   - Before: "When I was four years old, my grandfather convinced me that one word in the English language didn't exist. I've been living by it ever since."
   - After: "My grandfather died when I was a teenager. He was one of my favorite people in the entire world..."
   - Why: The hook explained the title and spoiled the story. Open on the loss to create emotional investment, and keep readers wondering what "no such word as can't" means until the middle of the article.

2. Story order
   - Before: Background, then sections built around props and events ("The carbon paper," "The truth").
   - After: The sequence of the user's notes: grandfather, drawing, carbon paper, the lie he talked the user out of, "I can't," the reply, the literal belief, the adult view, the reader.
   - Why: The user's notes already carry the narrative arc. Reorder sentences for clarity, not the story.

3. Headings removed (overcorrection)
   - Before: Section headings that did not fit the meaning.
   - After: No headings, then headings restored.
   - Why: The user objected to what the headings said, not to having them. Long articles need segments. Fix the specific complaint; do not strip structure.

4. Drawing section heading
   - Before: "The carbon paper," then "Learning to draw"
   - After: "A blank sheet"
   - Why: Carbon paper is a prop, not the subject. "Learning to draw" telegraphs the wrong takeaway ("he thought he couldn't draw"). "A blank sheet" is evocative and gives nothing away.

5. "The truth" section
   - Before: A separate "The truth" section with storybook dialogue ("He said, 'Well, that wouldn't be telling the truth. You can tell her you traced them...'")
   - After: Merged into the drawing section in plain prose: "I said to Grandad, 'I'm going to tell Mama I drew it all by myself!' He told me no, that wouldn't be the truth."
   - Why: The honesty lesson is a side moment, not a breaking point, and the scene did not need to read like a children's book.

6. Reveal heading
   - Before: "Taking him at his word" (reveal section)
   - After: "Two words"
   - Why: Ambiguous, punchy, and concise. The user later tied it to the text: "So I said the first two words that came to mind: 'I can't.'" Rejected: "the first two words I said" (reads as the user's first words ever) and "first thought... first two words" (repeats "first").

7. Formatting the key lines
   - Before: Plain text for the reveal and its reaction.
   - After: "There's no such word as "can't."" as a block quote, both times it appears; "I believed him. Literally." and other pivotal lines in bold.
   - Why: The user wanted the key line large and set apart; LinkedIn's quote block does that. The user asked for bold text a few pixels larger, but LinkedIn has no size control, so plain bold was the honest answer.

8. Agent-added reflective lines
   - Before: "On those days, I still hear my grandfather." and "You can't use a word that isn't there."
   - After: "And when I start thinking it's too late, Grandad's lesson is what I come back to." (user's line)
   - Why: Emotional beats must come from the user. The agent flagged its additions; the user replaced them. Later, "Grandad's lesson comes to mind" was rejected as passive and as an echo of "came to mind."

9. Why the child believed him
   - Before: "But through the eyes of a four-year-old, looking at my grandfather, I believed him." (user's edit)
   - After: "But through the eyes of a four-year-old whose grandfather was his world, I believed him. Literally."
   - Why: "My grandfather was my world" (from the user's notes) explains why a child took him literally. The agent proposed removing both commas around the clause; the user kept the one before "I believed," which closes the introductory phrase. The user was right.

10. Showing the belief
    - Before: (none)
    - After: "And when I heard someone else say "can't" in any way, I'd squeak, "Oooh, you said a bad word!" -- like it was profanity."
    - Why: A user-added detail. It is funny, specific, and proves the belief rather than asserting it. The strongest line in the piece.

11. Adult section heading
    - Before: "What stayed with me"
    - After: "Freehand"
    - Why: Too direct. Rejected: "Carbon copy" (clever, but pulls focus back to the carbon paper and doesn't convey the section), "Still four" (accurate but not clever), "Spell check" (good try). "Freehand" calls back to never learning to draw freehand and works as a metaphor for doing things on your own, without limits.

12. The adult admission
    - Before: "As an adult, of course, I have a much different perspective on what can and can't be done in life. But that isn't the point."
    - After: "Of course, I eventually realized that "can't" is a real word, but to this day, I believe that I can accomplish virtually anything that I put my mind, energy, and effort into"
    - Why: Admitting the word exists defuses the corniness of the slogan. "Virtually anything" is more believable than an absolute claim.

13. Vulnerability
    - Before: "My life is a good one, but there are days I feel I haven't lived up to my potential."
    - After: "I've made mistakes that I wish I could undo. I've made decisions that I'd make differently now. Life is good, but there are days I feel I haven't lived up to my potential."
    - Why: The user calibrated how much to reveal. The notes called the fuller version "overboard"; the agent kept a lighter version and the user set the final level.

14. Closing section heading
    - Before: "For you," then "Passing it on"
    - After: "You can."
    - Why: The agent suggested "Your blank sheet" (a callback to the first heading); the user chose "You can.", which pays off the article.

15. Age list
    - Before: "Maybe you're further along -- in your forties, fifties, sixties, or seventies -- and..."
    - After: "Whether you're early in your career or decades into it, starting fresh or starting over, it doesn't matter."
    - Why: Listing decades is wordy. Rejected along the way: a semicolon fragment ("...decades into it; starting fresh or starting over.").

16. Stock phrases (raised once, user's call)
    - Before: "Regardless of age, background, or circumstances, you have new heights to reach; you have more goals to accomplish, more potential to unleash."
    - After: (unchanged)
    - Why: The agent flagged "new heights" and "unleash potential" as stock motivational phrases. The user kept them. Raise a concern once, then respect the decision.

17. Closing question
    - Before: "Who was the person who believed in you before you believed in yourself -- and what did they tell you?"
    - After: "Who was the person that believed in you before you believed in yourself?"
    - Why: The user shortened it for punch. The agent noted "who" is standard for people and that the shorter form drops the prompt to share what was said; the user decided.

18. Hashtags
    - Before: "#Encouragement #Mindset #Family"
    - After: "#Encouragement #Mindset #Motivation"
    - Why: Keep hashtags aligned with the theme, not the cast.

19. Unrequested edits
    - Before: The agent applied fixes while reviewing.
    - After: Reverted; suggestions discussed only.
    - Why: "Review" means discuss. Edit the user's file only when asked.

20. Final checks
    - Why: A stray link pasted into the article, a missing blank line before a heading, and a mismatched curly apostrophe all surfaced in final reviews. Read the whole file every pass.

### Approved Post

Model this copy stylistically. It reflects the user's voice and approved wording.

```markdown
# There's No Such Word as "Can't"

My grandfather died when I was a teenager. He was one of my favorite people in the entire world, and no one brought me joy the way he did. His name was John Robert Higgins.

As a young child, I spent a lot of time with Grandad. There is one lesson he taught me that I'll always remember -- it became a part of who I am, my character, my identity.

## A blank sheet

When I was four or five years old, I loved to draw, and I still do. Back then I liked drawing animals -- a dog, a horse, a dolphin. Just line drawings, outlining each animal's features -- **but I wasn't great at it.**

So my grandfather introduced me to carbon paper. If you don't know what that is, it's a thin sheet of paper coated on one side with ink (usually carbon black). You can lay it on top of a blank sheet, and anything you write on the carbon paper transfers to the page beneath it. My grandfather showed me how I could take a picture from a coloring book, place it on top of the carbon paper, trace the outline, and the drawing would appear on the blank page underneath. I gave it a try, and it worked.

I traced so many animals onto one piece of paper, it looked like the character sheets used in gaming today. I was proud of myself and thought I had done a great job. I said to Grandad, "I'm going to tell Mama I drew it all by myself!" He told me no, that wouldn't be the truth. It burst my bubble a bit, but looking back, that was a good lesson, too.

## Two words

Then he suggested I try drawing the animals myself, without the carbon paper.

I had never been able to draw anything as good as what the carbon paper produced. So I said the first two words that came to mind: **"I can’t."**

And that's when my grandfather said:

> There's no such word as "can't."

As an adult, I understand now that it was motivation. It was encouragement. But through the eyes of a four-year-old whose grandfather was his world, **I believed him. Literally.** And although I knew I'd heard the word "can't" before, I figured I must have just had it wrong.

I don't think that I ever learned to draw those animals well as a child. But I can tell you that for a while, I truly believed the word "can't" did not exist. So I never used it. **I never said "I can't"** because there was no such word to say. And when I heard someone else say "can't" in any way, I'd squeak, "Oooh, you said a bad word!" -- like it was profanity. 

## Freehand

Of course, I eventually realized that "can't" is a real word, but to this day, I believe that I can accomplish virtually anything that I put my mind, energy, and effort into -- no matter how far out of reach it seems.

That matters a lot at this point in my life. I've made mistakes that I wish I could undo. I've made decisions that I'd make differently now. Life is good, but there are days I feel I haven't lived up to my potential. **And when I start thinking it's too late, Grandad's lesson is what I come back to.**

## You can.

So today, I want to pass it on to anyone who could use some encouragement.

Whether you're early in your career or decades into it, starting fresh or starting over, it doesn't matter. Regardless of age, background, or circumstances, you have new heights to reach; you have more goals to accomplish, more potential to unleash. **And you can do it, because...**

> There's no such word as "can't."

**Who was the person that believed in you before you believed in yourself?**

#Encouragement #Mindset #Motivation
```

### Companion post

Revisions, in order:

   - Before: "When I was four, my grandfather told me something I took literally... It's about the man who believed in me before I believed in myself..."
   - Why: Rejected as a prelude with no value, and wrong in focus. The post is about the lesson, not the grandfather.

2. Hook
   - Before: "When I was four, my grandfather told me something that wasn't true." then "...something no dictionary would agree with."
   - After: "When I was four, my grandfather told me something that didn't make sense. I believed it anyway, and it still shapes my approach to life." (user's line)
   - Why: "Wasn't true" was intriguing but implied he lied. The agent wrongly argued that "didn't make sense" contradicted the story; at four, it didn't make sense, and the child believed it anyway, which is the point. "My approach to life" was the user's choice over "anything that seems out of reach."

3. Call to action
   - Before: "If you've ever wondered whether it's too late... this one's for you." then "Give it a read, then tell me..." then "Fair warning: you may never hear one particular word the same way again."
   - After: "It might not make sense to you either, until it does."
   - Why: The first summarized the article; the second was too directive; the third fell flat. The approved line echoes the hook and promises a payoff without asking for anything.
