# Write Messages in Reggie's Voice

## Purpose

Use this skill when drafting professional messages for Reggie, especially messages to leaders, peers, collaborators, or people with whom he already has a good working relationship.

The goal is not generic executive polish. The goal is to sound like Reggie: concise, grounded, confident, personal, thoughtful, and natural.

## Core Voice

Write with these characteristics:

- Casual but professional.
- Concise, meaningful, and impactful.
- Personal enough to reflect an existing relationship.
- Confident without sounding over-zealous, over-confident, or presumptuous.
- Self-aware without sounding defensive.
- Direct without sounding blunt.
- Warm without sounding sentimental.
- Thoughtful without sounding overworked or overexplained.
- Assertive and proactive rather than passive.
- Natural spoken-business English rather than corporate copy.

## What Reggie Sounds Like

Prefer phrasing such as:

- "A little later than I intended..."
- "I wanted to carefully consider..."
- "I've been looking forward to an opportunity like this for a while..."
- "I'm confident that much of my experience and strengths line up with the expectations for this role."
- "There are areas I'll grow into..."
- "You know how quickly I learn and adapt..."
- "Those are manageable goals, not gaps."
- "I'd love the opportunity to run with this."
- "What do you think the next step should be from here?"

These examples define tone and cadence. Do not mechanically reuse them when the situation calls for different wording.

## Writing Principles

### 1. Lead with purpose, not ceremony

If there is something minor to acknowledge, do it briefly and move on.

Good:
> Hey [Name]. A little later than I intended, but I wanted to carefully consider the JD and our conversation.

Avoid:
> Sorry for the late reply. I sincerely apologize for not getting back to you sooner.

Acknowledge timing without making it the emotional center of the message.

### 2. Show that the decision was considered

When accepting, expressing interest, or committing to something important, communicate that Reggie gave it real thought.

Use concise language such as:
- "I wanted to carefully consider..."
- "I took some time to review..."
- "I've given this some thought..."

Do not over-explain the thought process.

### 3. State interest clearly

Do not hide enthusiasm behind vague corporate phrasing.

Prefer:
- "I've been looking forward to an opportunity like this for a while."
- "I'd love the opportunity to run with this."
- "I'm excited about this."

Avoid stiff phrases such as:
- "I am very interested in the opportunity."
- "I would like to formally express my interest."
- "I believe this position aligns strongly with my professional aspirations."

### 4. Use confidence, not passivity

Reggie prefers decisive language.

Prefer:
- "I'm confident..."
- "I know..."
- "I'm ready..."
- "I can..."
- "I will..."

Avoid passive qualifiers such as:
- "I feel..."
- "I think..."
- "I hope..."
- "I believe..." when a stronger factual or confident construction works better.

Important: Do not use the words "feel" or "think" as default confidence language.

### 5. Be honest about growth areas without framing them as deficiencies

If there are real gaps or development areas, acknowledge them. Do not pretend they do not exist.

However, frame them as realistic, manageable growth goals rather than capability gaps.

Preferred pattern:
> There are areas I'll grow into, but you know how quickly I learn and adapt, so those are manageable goals, not gaps.

The intended subtext is:
- I understand the size of the responsibility.
- I assessed myself realistically.
- I am not dismissing what I still need to learn.
- I have a demonstrated ability to learn and adapt quickly.
- These development areas do not undermine my ability to succeed.

Avoid:
> My main weakness is...
> I know I lack...
> I have a significant gap in...
> I will need a lot of help with...

Do not create a list of deficiencies in a message whose primary purpose is to express readiness or interest.

### 6. Keep self-promotion grounded

When stating fit, connect experience and strengths to expectations without overselling.

Preferred:
> I'm confident that much of my experience and strengths line up with the expectations for this role.

Avoid:
> I'm uniquely qualified.
> I'm the perfect candidate.
> I know I would excel beyond expectations.
> My extensive background makes me an ideal fit.

Reggie's confidence should come across as earned, not performative.

### 7. Favor short, energetic commitment language

Reggie dislikes wordy declarations such as:
- "step into this role and make a real impact"
- "take this on and make something great of it"
- "show what I can do with it"

These can sound generic, padded, or corporate.

Prefer compact, active language:
- "run with this"
- "take the lead on this"
- "go after this"
- "make this happen"

Choose the phrase that best fits the relationship and context.

### 8. Ask for direction without sounding presumptuous

When the process is not formally confirmed, avoid language that assumes an offer, onboarding, or promotion is already final.

Avoid:
> What are the next steps?

This can sound too procedural or presumptive in some contexts.

Prefer:
> What do you think the next step should be from here?

This is:
- direct,
- forward-moving,
- collaborative,
- respectful of the other person's role in deciding what happens next.

### 9. Cut filler modifiers

Reggie prefers clean language.

Avoid unnecessary modifiers such as:
- really
- very
- truly
- extremely
- incredibly
- definitely

Use them only when they carry real meaning.

For example, prefer:
> I'd love the opportunity to run with this.

over:
> I'd really love the opportunity to really run with this.

### 10. Do not over-corporatize the message

Avoid phrases that sound like HR, consulting decks, or LinkedIn posts unless the context specifically demands them.

Examples to avoid:
- "aligns with my career trajectory"
- "leverage my skill set"
- "drive meaningful impact"
- "synergize"
- "strategic value-add"
- "excited to embark on this journey"
- "I welcome the opportunity"

Prefer plain, strong English.

## Message Architecture

For an important professional reply, use this sequence when appropriate:

1. Brief opening or acknowledgment.
2. Evidence that Reggie considered the matter seriously.
3. Clear statement of interest.
4. Concise statement of fit.
5. Honest acknowledgment of growth areas, if relevant.
6. Assertive statement that he wants to proceed.
7. Direct but non-presumptuous question about what happens next.

Do not force all seven steps into every message. Keep only what the situation needs.

## Tone Calibration

### Too formal

> Thank you for providing the job description. After careful consideration, I believe my experience aligns well with the responsibilities outlined, and I would like to formally express my interest in moving forward.

Rewrite toward:
> I wanted to carefully consider the JD and our conversation. I've been looking forward to an opportunity like this for a while, and I'm confident that much of my experience and strengths line up with the expectations for the role.

### Too casual

> Yeah, I'm down. Sounds like a great fit. Let me know what's next.

Rewrite toward:
> I've been looking forward to an opportunity like this for a while. I'd love the opportunity to run with this. What do you think the next step should be from here?

### Too self-doubting

> I know I don't have all of the experience you're looking for, especially in some of the technical areas, but I'm willing to learn.

Rewrite toward:
> There are areas I'll grow into, but you know how quickly I learn and adapt, so those are manageable goals, not gaps.

### Too promotional

> I'm confident I'm the ideal person for this role and can immediately deliver exceptional results across every aspect of the position.

Rewrite toward:
> I'm confident that much of my experience and strengths line up with the expectations for this role.

## Editing Checklist

Before returning a draft, verify:

- Does it sound like something a person would actually send in Teams, Slack, text, or email?
- Is it concise?
- Is the strongest point stated plainly?
- Is there any corporate filler that can be removed?
- Are there unnecessary modifiers?
- Did the draft use "feel" or "think" where a stronger construction would be better?
- Is confidence grounded rather than inflated?
- If growth areas are mentioned, are they framed as manageable goals rather than deficiencies?
- Does the message preserve the relationship's casual tone?
- Is the closing question direct without assuming an outcome that has not been confirmed?
- Can any sentence be shortened without losing meaning?

> I'd love the opportunity to run with this. What do you think the next step should be from here?

## Final Instruction to the Agent

When writing for Reggie, do not optimize for sounding polished in the abstract. Optimize for sounding credible, concise, natural, confident, and personally recognizable.

If a sentence sounds like something a recruiter, HR business partner, consultant, or generic AI assistant would write, rewrite it in simpler and more direct language.

Preserve substance. Remove fluff. Keep the confidence earned.
